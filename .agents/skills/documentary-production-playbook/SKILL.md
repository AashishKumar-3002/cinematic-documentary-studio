# Documentary production playbook

Use for this project's English documentary channel. Keep creative freedom and the free-tools budget. Apply alongside the existing rendering and animation skills.

## Optional guidance

The user approved this as a useful reference, not a rigid recipe. Select, adapt, reorder or skip creative techniques according to the story. Do not force continuous movement, a single focal element, word-synced effects, a particular sequence of production steps or Shorts into every project. User direction, factual accuracy and clear communication take priority. Keep provenance and evidence distinctions even when changing the creative approach.

## User feedback to carry into future documentaries

These are the user's production priorities after viewing the Lake Nyos pilot, not optional claims from the tutorials. Apply them from the next film onward. The user explicitly requested learning only; do not rebuild or revise the existing pilot for this feedback.

1. **Make the writing and delivery engaging.** The pilot explained the facts but did not hold attention strongly enough. Build a compelling question, concrete stakes, cause-and-effect reveals and a satisfying payoff. Vary sentence length and delivery intention: curiosity, tension, discovery and reflection where the story supports them. Check whether the narration works when heard without the visuals. Voice C was preferred, but choosing that voice alone does not solve scripting or performance. Review a representative passage for pacing and emotional intent before generating the whole film. Keep the facts and their uncertainty intact.
2. **Make the visuals respond to what is said.** A matching static diagram or heading is not enough. When the narrator names an important object, relationship or change, direct attention to it at that spoken moment: highlight it, draw a circle or underline, trace a route, move an arrow, isolate a region or reveal the relevant detail. Choose the action that explains the point. Use the actual spoken-word timing and check the rendered result; proportional timing estimates need adjustment when an emphasis lands late or early. Leave enough time for the viewer to recognize the highlighted detail.
3. **Give motion and important moments sound.** Plan effects for scene changes, writing/drawing, reveals and important statements alongside the visuals. Match a writing cue to a line being drawn, a restrained transition sound to a scene move, or an accent to a meaningful reveal. Vary intensity so key moments stand out. Check the full sequence with sound, keeping the narration clear. A few generic chapter whooshes do not by themselves provide the interaction the user requested.

During storyboarding, record the spoken anchor, intended visual action and sound role for important beats. During review, check whether the emphasis actually helps explain the spoken point and whether the scene has a readable rhythm. Apply these priorities thoughtfully rather than adding an effect to every word.

Example: as the narration says the water rises, move the highlighted water parcel upward; on pressure dropping, shorten the pressure arrows; on bubbles forming, reveal those bubbles. Circle or accent the feedback loop when it becomes the key idea, with a timed sound cue if it strengthens the moment. This is a studio example for future work, not an instruction to revise Nyos.

Source: direct user feedback after the Lake Nyos full-film review, saved in [episode feedback](../../../projects/lake-nyos/feedback.md).

## Suggested method

1. Define the story question and payoff. Collect references for palette, typography, textures and camera language; design an original treatment.
2. Research claims and prepare an asset inventory tied to narration beats. Record provenance and usage rights. Give each asset a descriptive, accurate name.
3. Finalize narration and word timings before locking animation. Build a beat table containing time, spoken line, focal visual, on-screen text and evidence.
4. Give each beat one clear focal element. Use short, readable text and avoid decorative repetition of the same phrase. Accessibility captions can repeat narration.
5. Time meaningful highlights, map reveals and visual accents to the relevant spoken words. Use camera movement to reveal relationships. A continuous camera is an option; stillness and cuts remain available when clearer.
6. Match illustrative inserts to the episode's visual language and distinguish reconstructions from evidence. Reuse geometry and timing when comparing styles.
7. Inspect every beat and important transition. Revise with timestamp, defect and exact intended change. Check reading time and phone legibility before polishing effects.
8. Align selected sound cues to visual events and keep narration intelligible. Review the actual render before treating it as finished.

## Channel learning

The following are studio additions informed by YouTube analytics documentation, not growth results demonstrated by the tutorials:

- Make the title and thumbnail promise agree with the opening and delivered story.
- Inspect impressions, click-through rate and audience retention separately. Investigate drop-off timestamps; treat possible causes as hypotheses.
- Compare similar videos and traffic contexts. Test deliberate changes without promising a universal benchmark or fixed growth timeline.
- Build a recognizable design system while keeping each story original. Consider Shorts as separate experiments, not proof of long-form demand.

## Example

For a fictional bridge failure, establish its location on a map, emphasize the relevant inspection detail as it is narrated, then show a labelled schematic. If the detail disappears too quickly, extend that beat and remove competing labels before adding effects.

## Avoid

Generic floating text, unreadable speed, duplicated labels, unsupported factual diagrams, copied characters, indiscriminate effects and assumptions that old images are free to use. Do not treat automation, visual polish or daily uploads as a guarantee of views or earnings.

This skill authorizes no installations, account connections, paid services, scheduling or publishing. Use the user's existing authorization for any separate production action.
